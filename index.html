What “resistance” means

Resistance is just something your muscles have to push or pull against. The weight doesn't care where it comes from — a dumbbell, a machine, a stretchy band, or your own bodyweight in a push-up all count.

Training against resistance repeatedly is what makes a muscle adapt: it gets stronger and, over months, a bit bigger. That's the entire mechanism. Cardio trains your heart and lungs; resistance training trains your muscles and joints to handle load.

Free weights

Dumbbells, barbells. You balance the weight yourself, so more muscles share the work.

Machines

The machine holds the path steady for you. Easier to learn, harder to get hurt.

Bodyweight

Push-ups, squats, planks. No equipment needed, and plenty hard enough to start.

Bands

Resistance grows as the band stretches. Cheap, portable, gentle on joints.

Where to start

Start lighter than feels necessary. The first few weeks aren't about building muscle yet — they're about teaching your body the movement pattern (squat, hinge, push, pull, carry) so later, heavier weight is safe.

A sensible first month: full-body sessions, 2–3 times a week, with a rest day between. Five or six movements a session — one squat-type, one hinge (like a deadlift pattern), one push, one pull, one carry or core move — is a complete session. You don't need more than that to begin.

If a gym feels like too much at first, bodyweight at home (squats, push-ups, glute bridges, planks) is a legitimate way to spend the first couple of weeks. Form first, weight later.

The major muscle groups

The plans further down are built around these groups, so a quick map of them first. Most gym movements work several at once; the name just says which one does the most work.

Chest

Pushes weight away from the body. Worked by bench press, push-ups, chest fly.

Back

Pulls weight toward the body and holds posture upright. Worked by rows, pull-ups, lat pulldowns.

Shoulders

Lifts and rotates the arm. Shared by almost every push and pull you do. Worked directly by presses and raises.

Arms (biceps & triceps)

Biceps bend the elbow, triceps straighten it. Both get plenty of work from back and chest exercises; curls and pressdowns add extra, direct work.

Core

Keeps the spine stable under load. Works during almost every standing exercise, not only planks and crunches.

Legs (quads, hamstrings, glutes, calves)

The body's biggest muscles, usually needing the most total work. Worked by squats, deadlifts, lunges, leg press.

Setting up a week

A “set” is one block of reps done back to back, followed by a rest. “Reps” (repetitions) are how many times you do the movement inside that set. 3 sets of 10 means: do the movement 10 times, rest, repeat that two more times.

How those combine depends on the goal:

Goal Sets × reps Rest between sets
Learning the movement 2–3 × 10–15 60–90 sec
General strength & size 3–4 × 8–12 60–90 sec
Pure strength 3–5 × 3–6 2–3 min
Muscular endurance 2–3 × 15–20 30–45 sec

Heavier weight with fewer reps builds raw strength; lighter weight with more reps leans toward endurance and muscle size. Most beginners do best in the middle row.

Give the same muscle group at least one full day off before training it again — that rest is when the actual adaptation happens, not the workout itself.

Plans by level: minimum for health, extra for growth

Health authorities (WHO, and the ACSM/NSCA guidelines most gyms train on) recommend resistance work that covers every major muscle group at least twice a week. That's the minimum viable dose — enough to count as a real health benefit, even at low volume.

Beyond that minimum, research on training volume (summarised in meta-analyses by Schoenfeld and colleagues) finds a dose-response pattern: more weekly sets per muscle group, up to somewhere around 10–20, tends to build more muscle and strength than fewer — with returns tapering off past that. So the plans below show both numbers: a minimum that covers the health guideline, and an “extra” range if the goal is visible strength or size, not just general health.

Beginner

Full-body, 2–3 sessions a week, a rest day between. One movement per major group — this alone meets the minimum viable dose for general health.

  1. Chest press (machine or dumbbell)chest, shoulders, triceps2 × 10–15
  2. Seated row or lat pulldownback, biceps2 × 10–15
  3. Leg press or bodyweight squatquads, glutes2 × 10–15
  4. Light Romanian deadlift or hip hingehamstrings, glutes, lower back2 × 10–12
  5. Shoulder press (machine)shoulders2 × 10–15
  6. Plankcore2 × 20–30s

≈4–6 sets a week per muscle group across 2–3 sessions — this is the minimum viable range: it meets the WHO/ACSM health guideline, even before chasing extra growth.

Intermediate

3–4 sessions a week, split by body region (e.g. upper/lower, or push/pull/legs). Each group gets a second exercise and hits the research-backed range for extra growth, not just maintenance.

  1. Bench press + incline dumbbell presschest, shoulders, triceps3 × 8–12, 2 × 10–12
  2. Pull-up or lat pulldown + seated rowback, biceps3 × 8–12, 2 × 10–12
  3. Squat + leg curlquads, hamstrings, glutes3 × 8–12, 2 × 10–15
  4. Romanian deadlifthamstrings, glutes, lower back3 × 8–10
  5. Overhead press + lateral raiseshoulders3 × 8–12, 2 × 12–15
  6. Biceps curl + triceps pressdownarms2 × 10–15 each
  7. Hanging leg raise or cable crunchcore2–3 × 12–15

≈8–14 sets a week per muscle group — inside the range studies associate with greater muscle and strength gains than the beginner minimum.

Advanced

4–6 sessions a week, specialised by body part, usually periodised (volume and intensity planned in cycles). Near the upper end of what's actually been studied.

  1. 2–3 chest exercises (flat, incline, fly)chest≈12–16/wk
  2. 2–3 back exercises (rows, pulldowns, deadlift variations)back≈14–18/wk
  3. Legs split into quad-dominant and hip-dominant daysquads, hamstrings, glutes≈16–20/wk each
  4. Press + 2 raise variationsshoulders≈12–15/wk
  5. Direct biceps and triceps workarms≈10–14/wk each
  6. Varied core work across sessionscore≈8–12/wk

≈12–20 sets a week per muscle group — the upper end of the currently-studied range. Pushing much higher usually costs more recovery than it returns, and isn't well supported by the research.

(2009); and the dose-response meta-analysis on weekly training volume and muscle growth by Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger (2017), with later volume reviews (e.g. Baz-Valle et al., 2022) broadly supporting the same pattern.

Too heavy, or time to add weight?

Signs it's too heavy right now

  • Your form breaks down before the set is finished — back rounds, knees cave in, you swing the weight using momentum.
  • You can't complete the planned reps with control, even on the last rep.
  • Sharp or pinching pain (different from normal muscle burn or next-day soreness).

Signs it's time to add weight

  • You hit the top of your rep range with good form for two sessions in a row.
  • The last 1–2 reps feel challenging but controlled — not easy, not a fight.

When it's time, add a small amount — roughly 2.5–5% more than the current weight — not a big jump. This is called progressive overload: small, steady increases over weeks and months, not big leaps in a single session.

Ongoing joint pain, or pain that doesn't ease with rest, is worth a conversation with a doctor or physiotherapist rather than pushing through it.

Do & don't

Do

  • Warm up 5–10 minutes before lifting — light cardio plus a lighter set of the first movement.
  • Learn the movement with light weight before going heavier.
  • Track what you lifted last time, so you know what to aim for this time.
  • Sleep and eat enough — muscle is built during recovery, not during the set.
  • Ask gym staff to check your form on a new movement. That's a normal, expected question.

Don't

  • Don't chase heavier weight before the movement feels controlled.
  • Don't train the exact same muscles on back-to-back days with no rest.
  • Don't hold your breath through a heavy lift — breathe out on the effort.
  • Don't compare your week-one weights to someone else's year-three weights.
  • Don't skip every warm-up just to save five minutes.

Myths vs facts

Myth Eating around 2000 calories in a day is dangerous.

Fact 2000 calories is a commonly used reference amount for an average adult, not a danger threshold — actual needs vary by body size, activity, age and goal. Nobody drops dead at that number.

Myth Lifting weights turns fat into muscle.

Fact Fat and muscle are different tissues — one can't become the other. Training builds muscle; a calorie deficit over time reduces fat. The two can happen together, but they're separate processes.

Myth Lifting weights makes women “bulky.”

Fact Building a large amount of muscle takes years of deliberate training and eating, and depends heavily on hormones. Regular lifting typically gives a leaner, more defined look, not sudden bulk.

Myth “No pain, no gain” — a good workout should hurt.

Fact Muscle fatigue and mild next-day soreness are normal. Sharp pain during a movement is a stop signal, not a sign of progress — it's usually how injuries happen.

Myth You can target fat loss from one spot (like ab exercises for belly fat).

Fact Spot reduction isn't how the body works — fat loss happens across the whole body, in a pattern your genetics largely decide. Ab exercises strengthen abs; they don't specifically burn belly fat.

Myth If you stop lifting, muscle turns into fat.

Fact Again, different tissues can't convert into each other. What happens is muscle slowly shrinks from disuse while habits that once supported a deficit or maintenance may change too — two separate effects that just happen around the same time.

General information for healthy adults, not a personalised program or medical advice. Starting a new routine, or training around pain or a medical condition, is worth discussing with a doctor or qualified trainer first.
